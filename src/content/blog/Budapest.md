# Budapest in Two Days

A lot of you will pass through Budapest on the way to the wedding, and we'd push you to build a couple of days around it rather than just changing planes.

Budapest doesn't need much time to make an impression. Two days is enough to walk both sides of the Danube, eat very well, sit in a 16th-century Ottoman bathhouse, and understand why the city keeps pulling people back. Here's how we'd spend them.

That photo at the top is us on the Buda side back in 2018, with Parliament across the river — the view that sells the city, and one of the few free things in it.

## 🌉 Buda vs. Pest: know the split first

The Danube cuts the city into two very different halves that only merged into [one city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest) in 1873.

**Buda** (west bank) is hilly, quiet and residential — the Castle District, Fisherman's Bastion, leafy neighbourhoods. Beautiful, but sleepier at night.

**Pest** (east bank) is flat, dense and where everything happens — Parliament, Váci utca, the ruin bars, most of the restaurants and all of the nightlife. Grittier, more urban.

In practice you'll spend most of your time in Pest and cross into Buda for the sightseeing.

## 🏨 Where to stay (and where not to)

**Good bases:**

- **[District V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belv%C3%A1ros-Lip%C3%B3tv%C3%A1ros) (Belváros/Lipótváros)** — central, walkable, close to Parliament and the river. Pricier, but efficient.
- **[District VII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erzs%C3%A9betv%C3%A1ros) (Erzsébetváros)** — the Jewish Quarter, home of the ruin bars, full of restaurants and good mid-range hotels. Lively at night, which is either a plus or a minus depending on your tolerance for noise.

**Approach with caution:**

- **[District VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3zsefv%C3%A1ros) (Józsefváros), specifically right around Keleti station** — parts have gentrified, but the immediate station surroundings can feel rough at night and make for a poor first impression.
- **Far-out residential Buda (Districts XI, XII)** if you don't have a car — lovely, but you'll spend your trip commuting back into Pest.

## 💶 Money

Hungary's currency is the **forint (HUF)** — in the EU, but not the eurozone. Some places will take euros, usually at a worse rate than the market. Pay in HUF, use a card or cash from a local ATM, and treat euro-only pricing as a fairly reliable sign of a tourist markup.

## 🗣️ A few words of Hungarian

Hungarian is related to almost nothing you've ever spoken and nobody expects you to attempt it. English is widely spoken in the centre, so you'll be fine — but a couple of words go a long way, and people visibly warm up when you try.

- *Szia* — "hi" / "bye", informal (**SEE-ya**). The one you'll use most.
- *Jó napot!* — "good day", the polite version (**YOH NAH-pot**). Use it walking into a shop or café.
- *Köszönöm* — "thank you" (**KUH-suh-nuhm**). *Köszi* is the casual version.
- *Elnézést* — "excuse me" / "sorry" (**EL-nay-zaysht**). Works for squeezing past someone and for flagging a waiter.
- *Kérem* — "please" (**KAY-rem**).
- *Viszlát* — "goodbye" (**VEES-laht**).
- *Finom!* — "delicious!" (**FEE-nom**). Deploy after the goulash.

> 🥂 ***Egészségedre!*** — "cheers!" (**EGG-ay-shay-ged-reh**). It looks impossible and it mostly is. Nobody will mind if you mangle it — just make eye contact and commit.

One quirk worth knowing: some Hungarians won't **clink beer glasses**. The story goes that Austrian officers toasted with beer while executing Hungarian generals in 1849, so Hungarians swore off it for 150 years — an oath that technically expired in 1999. Historians think the tale is invented, and plenty of people clink away happily now, but if someone lifts their glass without touching yours, that's why.

## 🚋 Getting around

- **Public transport** — the metro, trams and buses are cheap, efficient and cover almost everything you'll want to see. Buy tickets from the machines or the [BudapestGO app](https://bkk.hu/en/budapestgo/) *before* boarding; inspectors do check.
- **Trams 2 and 4/6** are useful and genuinely scenic — Tram 2 runs along the Pest riverbank with Parliament views, and costs the same as any other ticket.
- **Taxis** — don't hail one on the street, especially around **Keleti and Nyugati stations** or the airport. Unlicensed drivers there have a real reputation for overcharging tourists. Use [Bolt](https://bolt.eu/en/) or the official [Főtaxi app](https://fotaxi.hu/en/) instead, and check the meter is running.

## 🏛️ What to see

Two days is enough for the highlights on both banks without rushing. Mix and match depending on your pace and what's open.

### In Pest

- **[Parliament Building](https://www.parlament.hu/en/web/house-of-the-national-assembly)** — one of the largest government buildings in the world, sitting right on the water. Free to admire from outside, or book an interior tour in advance through the [official ticket site](https://jegymester.hu) — they sell out, especially in summer. Early morning or around sunset for the best light and the fewest people.
- **[Váci utca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V%C3%A1ci_utca)** — the main pedestrian shopping street. Touristy, but a nice stroll and good for people-watching. Prices on the street itself run high; walk a block or two back for better food.
- **[Great Market Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Market_Hall)** — at the Pest end of Liberty Bridge. Lunch, souvenirs, and the paprika you should take home.
- **St. Stephen's Basilica** and the streets around it — worth a wander if you have time between the bigger sights, and best after dark when the whole façade is lit.
- **[New York Café](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=New+York+Cafe+Erzsebet+korut+9-11+Budapest)** — often called the most beautiful café in the world, and it makes a strong case: gilded ceilings, frescoes, chandeliers. Opened in 1894, once the haunt of the city's writers and poets. Go for coffee and cake rather than a meal. Pricey and touristy, but worth seeing once.
- **In the evening**, head into the Jewish Quarter for a ruin bar. **[Szimpla Kert](https://szimpla.hu) is a must** — the original one, a bombed-out building turned into a sprawling, plant-filled maze of mismatched furniture and art. Crowded and full of tourists, and still the one thing in Budapest's nightlife you shouldn't skip. Go early evening, before it peaks.
- **For dinner**, find a proper Hungarian bistro rather than a tourist-menu place on Váci utca — a few streets back in District V or VII is all it takes.

![St. Stephen's Basilica lit up at night above the Christmas market in the square](/szent_istvan.jpeg)
*St. Stephen's Basilica at night, with the Advent market filling the square in front of it. In September you'll get the building without the wooden huts and the crowds — still worth walking past after dark.*

### In Buda

- **[Fisherman's Bastion (Halászbástya)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisherman%27s_Bastion)** — a fairy-tale turreted terrace looking over the Danube and the Pest skyline. Go early to beat the tour groups. This is where the photo at the top of this page was taken.
- **[Buda Castle District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buda_Castle)** — wander the cobbled streets; the views back toward Parliament are the best in the city.
- **[Castle Hill Funicular (Budavári Sikló)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budav%C3%A1ri_Sikl%C3%B3)** — walk up if you're feeling energetic, ride it if you're not.

### Crossing between them

**[Chain Bridge (Széchenyi Lánchíd)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sz%C3%A9chenyi_Chain_Bridge)** — the iconic 19th-century suspension bridge linking the two halves. Walk it at golden hour for the best light on both banks. It's also the natural hinge between a Pest morning and a Buda afternoon, or the other way round.

A clean split: Parliament, Váci utca and the ruin bars on one day; the Castle District, Fisherman's Bastion and the Chain Bridge on the other. There's enough here to shuffle around based on opening hours, weather, or how much walking you're still up for.

## ♨️ The Turkish baths

Budapest sits on natural thermal springs, and its bathing culture goes back to the 16th century under **Ottoman occupation**. Several of the Turkish-built bathhouses are still in use — domed roofs, octagonal pools, hammam-style steam rooms. This is a genuine link to the city's Ottoman past, not just a spa day.

Build one into your two days, either as a slow morning or as the wind-down after a day on your feet.

- **[Rudas Thermal Bath](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Rudas+Thermal+Bath+D%C3%B6brentei+t%C3%A9r+9+Budapest)** — a real 16th-century Ottoman bathhouse with the original domed pool still in use, plus a modern wellness wing and a rooftop pool with skyline views. Probably the best balance of *authentically Turkish* and *actually comfortable*.
- **[Széchenyi Thermal Bath](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Sz%C3%A9chenyi+Thermal+Bath+Budapest)** — the big, famous, very yellow one in City Park. Not Ottoman (it's early 20th century), but the most iconic and most photographed, with huge outdoor pools where old men play chess in the water. Expect crowds.
- **[Lukács Baths](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Lukacs+Baths+Frankel+Leo+ut+25-29+Budapest)** — a local favourite, far less touristy than Széchenyi, with a slightly worn, old-school feel. Open daily, roughly 7am–7pm.
- **[Veli Bej Bath](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Irgalmasok+Veli+Bej+Bath+Budapest)** — a beautifully restored 16th-century hammam, smaller and quieter, with a cap on visitor numbers. The Turkish-bath experience without the crowds — though the cap means it does fill up, so go early.

> ⚠️ **Don't plan around Gellért.** The famous Art Nouveau baths closed in October 2025 for a full renovation and aren't expected to reopen until around 2028. Plenty of guides still list it.

> 🩴 Bring a towel and flip-flops if you have them — rentals cost extra — and note that a swim cap is required in some pools.

## 🍽️ Where to eat

**Hungarian bistros:**

- **[Hungarikum Bisztró](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Hungarikum+Bisztr%C3%B3+Steindl+Imre+u.+13+Budapest)** — District V, near Parliament. Cosy and unpretentious, consistently praised for its goulash and duck leg. Books up fast, so reserve ahead.
- **[Retek Bisztró](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Retek+Bisztro+N%C3%A1dor+u.+5+Budapest)** — District V. A bit more polished but still homely; goulash, duck leg, and a complimentary shot of pálinka to finish.
- **[Parasztkonyha](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Parasztkonyha+Restaurant+Okt%C3%B3ber+6.+u.+3+Budapest)** — District V. Grandma's-cooking energy with real depth of flavour; the Bakonyi beef stew with dumplings is the one to order. Wildly popular — book well ahead, or expect to queue half an hour at the weekend.
- **[Gettó Gulyás](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Gett%C3%B3+Guly%C3%A1s+Wesselenyi+utca+18+Budapest)** — Jewish Quarter. Traditional roots, retro-chic room; goulash, veal paprikash, stuffed cabbage.
- **[Paprika Vendéglő](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Paprika+Vendegl%C5%91+D%C3%B3zsa+Gy%C3%B6rgy+%C3%BAt+72+Budapest)** — near Heroes' Square. Big menu, reliably fair prices; the foie gras and the goulash both stand out. Popular enough with tourists that a reservation is worth it.
- **[Aranybástya](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Aranyb%C3%A1stya+Buda+Castle+Cs%C3%B3nak+u.+1+Budapest)** — inside Buda Castle, with a terrace over the whole city. Pricier — save it for one special dinner or a sunset drink.

**Lángos and street food:**

- **[Lángosom](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=L%C3%A1ngosom+Wesselenyi+utca+35+Budapest)** — Wesselényi utca, Jewish Quarter. Fried fresh in front of you, and less touristy than most.
- **[Retro Lángos](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Retro+L%C3%A1ngos+Budapest+Bajcsy-Zsilinszky+ut+25)** — central, crispy, generous with the toppings.
- **[Great Market Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Market_Hall)** — a quick lángos or chimney cake alongside the produce and paprika shopping.
- **[Street Food Karavan](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Street+Food+Karavan+Kazinczy+u.+18+Budapest)** — food-truck court in the Jewish Quarter, right next to the ruin bars. Ideal when everyone in your group wants something different.

**Bars, beyond Szimpla:**

- **[Instant-Fogas](https://www.google.com/maps/search/?api=1&query=Instant+Fogas+Ruin+Bar+Budapest)** — a sprawling multi-room ruin bar complex with the same energy as Szimpla and slightly fewer tourists.

Almost all of the above sits in Districts V and VII, so most of your eating and drinking can happen on foot without backtracking.

## 🥘 What to eat

A few of these aren't exclusively Hungarian. Thanks to a shared Transylvanian and Carpathian history, several have close Romanian cousins — we've flagged the ones where the overlap is genuinely interesting. (You'll meet some of them again at the wedding.)

**Mains, stews and soups:**

- **[Gulyás (goulash)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goulash)** — the hearty beef-and-paprika soup-stew the country is known for. Order it at a proper bistro, not a chain, for the real thing.
- **[Paprikás csirke (chicken paprikash)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paprikash)** — chicken in a creamy paprika sauce, usually with *nokedli*, small egg dumplings.
- **Pörkölt** — the thicker stew that goulash actually descends from: meat braised in paprika and onions, without the soupiness of gulyás.
- **Töltött káposzta (stuffed cabbage)** — cabbage rolls of minced meat and rice. **Also Romanian:** *sarmale* is the direct equivalent, and arguably the more iconic version of the two. You will be eating these at the wedding.
- **Halászlé (fisherman's soup)** — a spicy, paprika-forward fish soup, especially common in the Danube and Tisza river towns.
- **Hortobágyi palacsinta** — a savoury crêpe filled with minced meat in paprika sauce. **Also Romanian:** *clătite* are the cousin, though usually sweet and rarely stuffed with meat.
- **Túrós csusza** — pasta tossed with farmer's cheese (*túró*), sour cream and bits of bacon.
- **Lecsó** — stewed paprika peppers, tomatoes and onions, often with sausage or egg stirred through. More of a home-cooking staple than a restaurant dish.
- **Bableves (bean soup)** — thick, usually made with smoked meat.
- **Hideg meggyleves (cold sour cherry soup)** — a chilled, sweet-sour summer soup that throws most first-timers. **Also Romanian:** the near-identical *supă de vișine* divides children and adults down the middle — ask any Romanian who was served it in kindergarten.
- **Kolbász** — Hungarian sausage, heavily seasoned with paprika.
- **Hurka** — traditional blood or liver sausage. Not for everyone, but a genuine taste of Hungarian charcuterie.

**Snacks and street food:**

- **[Lángos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L%C3%A1ngos)** — deep-fried dough with sour cream and grated cheese, garlic optional. **Also Romanian:** *langoș* is hugely popular there too, especially in Transylvania. The traditional topping is sour cream, cheese and maybe garlic — the loaded, burger-style versions at tourist stands are a modern gimmick.
- **[Kürtőskalács (chimney cake)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%BCrt%C5%91skal%C3%A1cs)** — a thin sheet of dough rolled around a cylinder, roasted over coals and coated in sugar with walnut or cinnamon. **Not originally Hungarian:** it's a Transylvanian (Székely) pastry, arguably more native to Romania than to Hungary. Skip the ones stuffed with ice cream or Nutella like a cone — tourist invention, not the real thing.
- **Túró Rudi** — a sweetened farmer's cheese bar coated in chocolate, sold in every supermarket. An everyday snack rather than a restaurant dish, and the cheapest good souvenir you'll find. Grab a few from any Spar or Tesco before you fly home.
- **[Paprika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paprika)** — the defining spice of the whole cuisine. Buy some at the Great Market Hall and take it home.

**Desserts:**

- **Mákos (poppyseed pastries)** — poppyseed is a staple of Hungarian bakeries; look for *mákos guba* or poppyseed strudel.
- **[Rétes (strudel)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strudel)** — apple, cherry or poppyseed. Classic Central European bakery fare.
- **Dobos torte** — layered sponge with chocolate buttercream and a signature caramel top, invented right here in Budapest.
- **Somlói galuska** — sponge cake trifle soaked in rum, layered with chocolate sauce and whipped cream.
- **Krémes** — a thick slab of vanilla custard between two sheets of puff pastry, dusted with icing sugar. Deceptively simple and very hard to stop at one. **Also Romanian:** *cremeș* (or *cremșnit*) is the exact same cake and a Transylvanian bakery staple — Cătă grew up on them.
- **Beigli** — a rolled pastry with a tight spiral of walnut or poppyseed filling, baked at Christmas and Easter. **Also Romanian:** a close cousin of *cozonac cu nucă*, though the Romanian version is more bread-like.
- **Marzipan (marcipán)** — taken seriously here in a way it isn't in most places, and it turns up as the decoration on top of half the cakes in any decent *cukrászda*. **Szamos** is the name to look for, with cafés and shops around the city and a marzipan museum out in Szentendre, where the sculptures are the whole point. Boxed marzipan also makes a good, sturdy souvenir.

![A krémes and a marzipan-topped slice with two coffees on a café table at Ruszwurm](/dessert_ruszrum.jpeg)
*A krémes on the right, a marzipan-topped slice on the left, and two coffees to thaw out with — us warming up on a winter afternoon at **Ruszwurm** in the Castle District. Sadly it's gone: the 1827 confectionery was forced to close in August 2025 after a long legal fight with the district over its lease. The interior is being kept intact and the council has said a new operator will be chosen by tender, so it may yet come back — but don't plan a trip around it. Treat this as a picture of what to order, not where.*

**Drinks:**

- **[Pálinka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C3%A1linka)** — strong fruit brandy, plum or apricot, taken as a shot before or after a meal. **Also Romanian:** the near-identical *palincă* / *țuică* is a point of friendly rivalry over who got there first, especially in Transylvania. Do not raise this at the wedding unless you want a long evening.
- **[Unicum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicum)** — a bitter herbal liqueur, 40-odd herbs aged in oak, and nothing like pálinka. Created in 1790 by Dr. József Zwack as a digestive for the Habsburg court; legend has Emperor Joseph II tasting it and declaring *"Das ist ein Unikum!"* — hence the name. Still made by the Zwack family in Budapest. Round green bottle, gold cross, thoroughly acquired taste.

## 🛒 Groceries

If you want to self-cater, stock up on snacks, or grab cheap souvenirs like Túró Rudi and paprika: the common chains are **Tesco**, **Penny Market** and **Lidl**, plus **Spar** (everywhere centrally — look for Spar Express and Interspar), **Aldi**, and the local **CBA** on residential corners. Bigger hypermarkets like **Auchan** sit out toward the city edges. For a quick central stop, a Spar or Tesco Express in District V or VII is never far.

## ✈️ Flying in

**[Budapest Ferenc Liszt International Airport (BUD)](https://www.bud.hu/en)** is the only major airport, about 16 km southeast of the centre, and it's well connected by budget and full-service carriers from across Europe. Ignore the taxi touts in arrivals and take the bus:

- **100E Airport Express** — direct to Deák Ferenc tér in about 40 minutes, running 24/7. It needs its own dedicated ticket (2,500 HUF, buyable by card on board), and **ordinary single tickets and travelcards are not valid on it** — the single most common way visitors get fined here.
- **200E** — the slower, cheaper local bus to Kőbánya-Kispest, where you pick up the M3 metro. Takes a normal ticket.

Details and current fares via [BKK](https://bkk.hu/en/travel-information/airport-express/).

## 🇷🇴 Carrying on to Oradea

The whole reason many of you are here: [Oradea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oradea) is a short hop across the border into Transylvania, and there are three sensible ways to do it.

- **Bus** — the best balance of cost and convenience. [FlixBus](https://global.flixbus.com/bus-routes/bus-budapest-oradea) and others run several direct services a day from Népliget bus station, taking roughly 3.5–5 hours depending on the route, from around €12–30. Compare everything on [Omio](https://www.omio.com/buses/budapest/oradea).
- **Train** — doable, but usually requires a change at Püspökladány and takes around 5 hours. Run by [Hungarian Railways (MÁV)](https://www.mavcsoport.hu/en).
- **Driving** — fastest at roughly 3–3.5 hours for the ~257 km, and the right call if you want a car on the other side.
- **Private transfer** — **[Airbus Trans](https://airbustrans.ro/contact/)**, an Oradea-based company doing airport transfers and minibus hire. Email **office@airbustrans.ro** or call **(+40) 744 576 589** to reserve. Worth it if you're arriving with luggage, travelling as a group, or landing at an hour the buses don't cover. Their site is in Romanian, but write to them in English and they'll reply — and if you get stuck, [message Cătă](/contact).
